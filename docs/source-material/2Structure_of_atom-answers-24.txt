2. An orbital is the space in an atom where there is a high probability (~95%) of finding an electron
according to quantum theory & is our current model for the structure of an atom. Electrons in orbitals
do not act the same way as the macro-world. The electrons move from one place to another without
having been between those two points. You cannot know with certainty where an electron will appear
next or where it was previously. These orbitals have specific shapes (s =spherical; p = dumbbell, etc).
Electrons in these orbitals have higher energy as the average distance from the nucleus increases.
An orbit is a specific path around a central object. Because of this, if an electron were in an orbit, it
would be possible to know where an electron is going & where it has been. This is an outdated model
for the structure of an atom.
One orbital can have a maximum of 2 electrons. An orbital can have a minimum of 0 (zero) electrons.
3. Electrons are not massless. Their mass is about 1/1836 of a proton or neutron. Therefore, when you
add the protons + neutrons + electrons, the electrons have such low mass that they do not add mass
when you consider significant figures. For example, consider Carbon with 6 protons, 6 neutrons & 6
electrons:
6*(1 amu/proton) + 6*(1amu/neutron) + 6*(5 x 10-4 amu/electron) = 6.003 amu = 6 amu
4. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons and therefore different
masses. The identity of an element is determined by the number of protons, so changing the number of
neutrons does not change the element.
5. No, the masses on the periodic table do not correlate to a specific isotope. Instead, the masses are a
weighted average of all of the different isotopes found on Earth. The weighting is calculated by their
natural abundance.
6. No, but it would probably be close. It is likely a different planet might have different isotope
abundance which would change the average mass.
